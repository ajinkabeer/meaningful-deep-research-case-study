# Untangling the Causes of Third-Place Closures: Delivery Apps vs. Rent & Labor Costs

## 1. Impact of Delivery App Penetration on Independent Venue Closures (U.S. Data)  
Recent research provides evidence that the rise of food **delivery apps has significantly increased closure rates for independent restaurants**, even after accounting for local economic factors. A detailed study of the U.S. restaurant industry from 2012–2018 found that **each 1% increase in food-delivery platform penetration led to a roughly 1.6% increase in the likelihood of restaurants closing】 ([mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Raj-Manav_When-Delivery-Comes-to-Town.pdf#:~:text=distribution%20platforms%20in%20the%20restaurant,Through%20this)). Importantly, the researchers controlled for market differences (using techniques like instrumental variables based on local infrastructure) to isolate the effect of delivery apps from other factors like urban location or consumer demand ([mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Raj-Manav_When-Delivery-Comes-to-Town.pdf#:~:text=of%20delivery%20platforms%20across%20different,6%20percent)). The conclusion was clear: **the entry and growth of platforms such as Grubhub, DoorDash, and Uber Eats correlated with higher exit rates for restaurants** – beyond what would be expected from normal business churn or local cost trends ([mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Raj-Manav_When-Delivery-Comes-to-Town.pdf#:~:text=distribution%20platforms%20in%20the%20restaurant,Through%20this)).

The mechanism appears to be that **delivery apps intensify competition and erode margins for brick-and-mortar eateries** ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=complicated,of%20the%202010s%20that%20truly)) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=across%20much%20larger%20geographic%20areas%2C%E2%80%9D,Marketing%20services%20to)). By making it easy for customers to order from *any* restaurant in a wide area, these platforms eliminate the traditional advantage of proximity. That means **small independent cafés and restaurants suddenly face many more competitors, while also paying commission fees of 15–30% on each order to the apps ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=for%20restaurants,these%20costs%20imposed%20by%20the)) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=Behind%20the%20scenes%2C%20delivery%20platforms,because%20they%20trust%20them%2C%20not))**. Many independent venues operate on thin profit margins; losing a chunk of revenue to delivery commissions can quickly turn a sustainable business into one that’s financially untenable. **Wharton analysis confirms that delivery-app expansion “significantly increases” the risk of restaurant failure** as less-efficient and less-established eateries struggle to survive the squeeze on profits ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=complicated,online%20instead%20of%20calling%20restaurants)) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=tougher%2C%20not%20all%20restaurants%20are,delivery%20platforms%20have%20reshaped%20how)). In short, **cities with higher food-app usage have seen faster shake-outs of independent restaurants, even when underlying costs are accounted for** by researchers’ models.

Evidence from industry data also suggests that as delivery app usage grew, **in-person dining sales were cannibalized at an alarming rate**. A 2020 study using Visa transaction data across U.S. ZIP codes estimated that **only 30–50 cents of each dollar spent on delivery was *new* spending – the rest directly replaced traditional in-person restaurant sales ([www.web.stanford.edu](https://www.web.stanford.edu/~leinav/teaching/Collison.pdf#:~:text=disrupted%20the%20traditionally%20o%20ine,that%20there%20is%20an%20increasing))**. This finding of *50–70% sales cannibalization* indicates that high app adoption can dramatically reduce foot traffic and on-site revenue for local eateries. **What’s more, restaurant owners themselves report seeing profits decline when delivery orders rise** – one restaurateur noted, *“We know for a fact that as delivery increases, our profitability decreases”* ([scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/69766b95-d32e-413b-9e77-72f17637b090/content#:~:text=both%20anecdotal%20,Sometimes%20it%20seems%20like)). Taken together, these data points show that **in U.S. markets where third-party delivery apps became popular, independent restaurants and cafes experienced a measurable uptick in closures, above and beyond what local rent or wage pressures alone would predict**. The effect has been especially hard on newer, smaller establishments without an existing loyal customer base ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=tougher%2C%20not%20all%20restaurants%20are,delivery%20platforms%20have%20reshaped%20how)).

## 2. Delivery Platforms vs. Cost Pressures: Parsing the Causes of Restaurant Decline  
**It is challenging to pinpoint an exact percentage share of closures caused *solely* by delivery-app/ghost-kitchen competition versus rent and labor costs**, because these factors often **interact**. However, **industry experts and economists have begun to assess their relative impact**:

- **Rising Costs as a Primary Driver:** Broadly, **surveys of restaurant operators show that higher operating costs (rent, food, and labor) remain the *top-cited reasons* for closures and financial distress** – far more than competition from delivery. For example, in a 2021 industry survey, 30% of restaurants said **rent was their single greatest source of financial strain and another 30% cited labor costs**, whereas only *1%* of restaurants chose “other causes” (a category that would include competition or delivery issues) as their top strain ([www.soocial.com](https://www.soocial.com/restaurant-failure-rate-statistics/#:~:text=not%20eating%20out%20at%20restaurants,for)). Likewise, by 2022–2023 the National Restaurant Association found **soaring costs in every major expense category** (with food and labor costs each up ~35% since 2019) that squeezed margins and led many independents to operate at a loss ([restaurant.org](https://restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/analysis-commentary/elevated-costs-continue-to-pressure-restaurant-profitability/#:~:text=major%20categories%20since%202019%20%E2%80%93,and%20menu%20prices%20didn%E2%80%99t%20rise)). In short, **the majority of independent restaurant closures in recent years can be attributed to traditional economic pressures** – *especially steep increases in rents, wages, and food prices* – which eroded already-thin profit margins ([www.soocial.com](https://www.soocial.com/restaurant-failure-rate-statistics/#:~:text=not%20eating%20out%20at%20restaurants,for)) ([restaurant.org](https://restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/analysis-commentary/elevated-costs-continue-to-pressure-restaurant-profitability/#:~:text=estimates%20that%20input%20costs%20posted,restaurants%2C%20customer%20traffic%20is%20still)). 

- **Delivery and Ghost Kitchens as an Accelerating Factor:** At the same time, **delivery platform competition has emerged as a potent *secondary* factor that can hasten closures in certain markets**. Urban economists note that delivery apps create new “invisible” competition and siphon customers away from on-premises dining ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=complicated,of%20the%202010s%20that%20truly)) ([www.web.stanford.edu](https://www.web.stanford.edu/~leinav/teaching/Collison.pdf#:~:text=the%20staggered%20entry%20of%20online,restaurants%27%20revenues%20but%20a%20decrease)). The effect is **particularly pronounced in dense, high-cost metro areas** where app adoption is highest. In those markets, online delivery can be the *tipping point*: restaurants already stretched by rent or labor expenses may lose enough business to apps and **virtual competitors that they can’t survive ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=complicated,of%20the%202010s%20that%20truly)) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=Behind%20the%20scenes%2C%20delivery%20platforms,because%20they%20trust%20them%2C%20not))**. The Wharton study above is one of the first to **quantify the effect of delivery platforms** and found that even *after controlling for local economic conditions*, the spread of delivery apps caused a statistically significant rise in restaurant exit rates ([mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Raj-Manav_When-Delivery-Comes-to-Town.pdf#:~:text=distribution%20platforms%20in%20the%20restaurant,Through%20this)). This suggests that **a non-trivial share of recent independent restaurant failures – especially post-2015 – is linked to the new competitive pressures from food delivery platforms**.

- **Interplay of Factors:** It’s important to note that these causes are not mutually exclusive. **Delivery apps and ghost kitchens often *exacerbate* cost pressures rather than replace them**. For instance, paying 20–30% commissions on deliveries effectively acts like an added “tax” on revenue ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=Behind%20the%20scenes%2C%20delivery%20platforms,because%20they%20trust%20them%2C%20not)), which **worsens the burden of high labor and rent costs**. Thus, an independent café in a high-rent city neighborhood might survive *either* a 30% rent hike *or* the loss of some eat-in customers to delivery—but *both together* can be fatal. An industry analyst from Deloitte observed that **many independent restaurants that failed in the late 2010s did so due to a combination of unsustainable operating costs and the competitive loss of sales to more efficient online-delivery models** (for example, fast-casual chains with robust delivery, or nearby ghost kitchens) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=complicated,of%20the%202010s%20that%20truly)) ([magazine.wharton.upenn.edu](https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/fall-winter-2025/are-food-delivery-apps-hurting-restaurants/#:~:text=across%20much%20larger%20geographic%20areas%2C%E2%80%9D,Marketing%20services%20to)). In summary, **the consensus among analysts is that *most* third-place closures in the past decade have been driven by rising costs (rent, wages, ingredients), while delivery apps and ghost kitchens are a significant *but secondary* cause that can accelerate closures in certain high-delivery, high-cost markets**.

*(*If data appear conflicting on this point, it is because causation is hard to definitively assign. For example, the **COVID-19 pandemic** both spurred massive adoption of delivery apps *and* created extraordinary cost and labor challenges for restaurants. Many closures in 2020–2021 were officially due to pandemic restrictions or staffing shortages, yet **the same period saw an explosion of ghost kitchens and delivery usage** that fundamentally changed consumer behavior ([www.cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/17/how-ghost-kitchens-went-from-1-trillion-hype-to-a-struggling-business-model.html#:~:text=that%20today%20with%20the%20decline,food%20business%20to%20expand%20to)) ([www.web.stanford.edu](https://www.web.stanford.edu/~leinav/teaching/Collison.pdf#:~:text=Delivery%20transactions%20made%20up%20six,revenue%2C%20they%20also%20create%20the)). Any analysis of cause must account for such overlaps. So far, **no single study has apportioned an exact percentage to “delivery competition” vs “rent/labor” across all closures**, but multiple sources agree that **traditional cost pressures remain the primary broad cause, with app-based competition being an important new contributor to incremental closures**.)*

## 3. Ghost Kitchen Proliferation and Third-Place Displacement  
**“Ghost kitchens” – delivery-only food facilities – expanded dramatically after 2020**, raising concerns about their impact on local third places. By one estimate the number of ghost kitchen outlets worldwide **surpassed 50,000 in 2022**, with the *global* ghost kitchen market reaching $48.8 billion ([gitnux.org](https://gitnux.org/ghost-kitchen-statistics/#:~:text=ghost%20kitchen%20market%20valued%20at,8%20billion%20in%202023)). In the U.S., **ghost kitchens handled roughly 10% of all restaurant orders by 2022 ([wifitalents.com](https://wifitalents.com/ghost-kitchen-statistics/#:~:text=growing%20at%20a%20CAGR%20of,this%20report%20goes%20through%20a))**, and the U.S. ghost kitchen sector grew to an estimated $5+ billion in revenue in just a few years ([www.cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/17/how-ghost-kitchens-went-from-1-trillion-hype-to-a-struggling-business-model.html#:~:text=however%2C%20that%20hasn%27t%20gone%20along,Consumers%20complained%20about%20the)). Major restaurant chains jumped on the trend (e.g. Wendy’s and Chili’s opened virtual brands), while countless independent “virtual restaurants” also launched online-only brands via apps ([www.cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/17/how-ghost-kitchens-went-from-1-trillion-hype-to-a-struggling-business-model.html#:~:text=industry%20is%20falling%20short%20of,mortar)). 

**Geographically, ghost kitchen adoption has been concentrated in dense urban markets.** In fact, about **80% of ghost-kitchen customers are urban dwellers ([gitnux.org](https://gitnux.org/ghost-kitchen-statistics/#:~:text=52,higher%20on%20weekends))** (often younger consumers), and ghost operators strategically locate their kitchens for delivery efficiency. A recent spatial analysis of ghost kitchens found they exhibit **“distinct clustering” in lower-rent, less-visible commercial areas that still allow quick access to customer hubs ([weipengdeng.github.io](https://weipengdeng.github.io/publications/2026-digital/#:~:text=study%20maps%20the%20spatial%20distribution,storefront%20exposure%20for%20access%20and)) ([weipengdeng.github.io](https://weipengdeng.github.io/publications/2026-digital/#:~:text=compares%20them%20with%20traditional%20and,platforms%2C%20and%20public%20agencies%20by))**. In other words, **ghost kitchens trade off prime storefronts for cheaper rents and proximity to delivery demand** – for example, setting up in industrial districts or edge-of-neighborhood locations where space is affordable, then serving customers in the surrounding high-density neighborhoods. This **“delivery-oriented siting” pattern ([weipengdeng.github.io](https://weipengdeng.github.io/publications/2026-digital/#:~:text=compares%20them%20with%20traditional%20and,platforms%2C%20and%20public%20agencies%20by))** means that some neighborhoods may **lose traditional restaurants on their main streets while seeing a rise in off-site kitchens hidden in warehouse spaces**.

The question of whether **areas with more ghost kitchens have higher third-place closure rates and greater social isolation** is still being studied. There is **anecdotal evidence and logical reasons to suspect a connection**, but **hard data are limited.** We do know that **neighborhoods with vibrant delivery markets often face higher brick-and-mortar attrition ([mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Raj-Manav_When-Delivery-Comes-to-Town.pdf#:~:text=distribution%20platforms%20in%20the%20restaurant,Through%20this))**, as described earlier. And we know **ghost kitchens surged precisely in areas where the pandemic had shuttered many dine-in establishments**, filling a void for takeout options ([www.cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/17/how-ghost-kitchens-went-from-1-trillion-hype-to-a-struggling-business-model.html#:~:text=that%20today%20with%20the%20decline,are%20pivoting%20to%20a%20new)). For example, **New York City, Los Angeles, Chicago, and other major metros – which also have some of the highest delivery app usage – witnessed waves of independent restaurant closures during 2019–2021** (due to the pandemic and cost pressures) even as *new* virtual restaurants sprang up to serve those same customers via apps ([www.web.stanford.edu](https://www.web.stanford.edu/~leinav/teaching/Collison.pdf#:~:text=the%20staggered%20entry%20of%20online,restaurants%27%20revenues%20but%20a%20decrease)) ([www.web.stanford.edu](https://www.web.stanford.edu/~leinav/teaching/Collison.pdf#:~:text=Delivery%20transactions%20made%20up%20six,revenue%2C%20they%20also%20create%20the)). However, **no comprehensive study has yet correlated ghost kitchen density per neighborhood with specific social isolation metrics**. Ghost kitchens only rose to prominence in the past 3–4 years, so research is still catching up. 

That said, the broader pattern is worrying: **communities that lose physical third places tend to experience reduced face-to-face interaction and increased loneliness ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=reach%20far%20beyond%20losing%20a,effects%20can%20worsen%20health%20outcomes)) ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=they%20cannot%20fully%20replace%20in,places%20will%20require%20collaboration%20among))**. If ghost kitchens are *both a symptom and a cause* of third-place decline in an area – a symptom, because they often appear when traditional restaurants have trouble affording rent, and a cause, because their presence diverts even more business away from remaining dine-in venues – then **we would indeed expect higher ghost-kitchen penetration to correlate with *fewer* open social venues and potentially higher social isolation.** This is a hypothesis that still needs direct verification. Early data on third-place losses (not specific to ghost kitchens) already show that **the disappearance of community gathering spots has been most acute in economically distressed or rapidly changing neighborhoods ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=experienced%20significant%20closures%2C%20particularly%20between,limited%20transportation%20infrastructure%20makes%20it)) ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=losses%20were%20especially%20harmful%20in,Third%20places%20play%20an%20essential))**. These tend to be the same areas where alternative options like delivery services or virtual kitchens either rush in to meet demand or where **residents are left with fewer convenient food options at all**. 

In summary, **ghost kitchens have proliferated fastest in urban centers and “delivery-friendly” zones, often coinciding with locales where many small restaurants and cafes were closing**. While we lack a formal study directly tying ghost kitchen *density* to an uptick in closure rates or loneliness indicators, **the circumstantial evidence suggests that they thrive in markets where brick-and-mortar third places are under heavy strain**. The net impact is likely a **further weakening of social infrastructure** in those neighborhoods – a trend that researchers are now beginning to investigate more closely.

## 4. Social and Civic Impacts of Losing Independent Cafés, Bars, and Other Third Places  
It is well documented that **when local third places shut down, communities suffer declines in social engagement and cohesion**. Third places (a term coined by sociologist Ray Oldenburg) contribute heavily to **“social infrastructure” – the web of informal interactions and relationships that bind communities together ([escholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/6dn5f64t#:~:text=the%20potential%20consequences%20for%20the,first%20place)) ([escholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/6dn5f64t#:~:text=recreation%20and%20community%20centers%2C%20sites,Layton%2C%202019%3B%20Klinenberg%2C%202018))**. When a coffee shop or pub closes, people **lose a regular venue for *casual conversation, neighborly interactions, and community activities* ([escholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/6dn5f64t#:~:text=1415%20Washington%20Heights%2C%20Ann%20Arbor%2C,support%20wellbeing%20through%20mechanisms%20of)) ([escholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/6dn5f64t#:~:text=the%20potential%20consequences%20for%20the,first%20place))**. A new nationwide study by researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder found that the **widespread disappearance of third places across the U.S. (especially from 2010 to 2021) is a growing public health concern ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=Yue%20Sun%2C%20a%20postdoctoral%20research,closures%20happen%20because%20of%20long)) ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=reach%20far%20beyond%20losing%20a,effects%20can%20worsen%20health%20outcomes))**. This research showed that **neighborhoods which suffered long-term closures of gathering places ended up with measurably lower social connectivity and greater loneliness**. As one of the authors put it, *“When they close, people interact less… they feel more isolated and lose some sense of belonging”* ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=they%20cannot%20fully%20replace%20in,places%20will%20require%20collaboration%20among)). In practical terms, the loss of a coffee shop might mean fewer chance encounters with neighbors, and the closure of a local bar or bookstore can mean the end of book clubs, trivia nights, or community meet-ups that kept people socially active. Over time, those losses **translate into **reduced civic engagement and weaker community bonds** – residents have fewer opportunities to organize, share information, or simply talk about local issues.

There are also **specific case studies linking third-place closures to declines in social well-being.** In the UK, for example, the rapid disappearance of neighborhood pubs has coincided with rising loneliness indicators. **More than 2,000 pubs have closed in the UK since 2020, and in 2025 they were disappearing at a rate of *eight per week*** ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=save%20the%20British%20pub%20sector,For%20many%2C%20especially)) ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=campaign%20that%20fights%20to%20keep,urgent%20need%20for%20the%20government)). **In a recent British survey, 33% of people said either they or someone they know became *more lonely* directly due to the loss of a local pub ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=levels,or%20%E2%80%9Cvery%20important%E2%80%9D%20for%20their))**. Moreover, **two-thirds of respondents said pubs are “vital” in fighting social isolation in their community ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=levels,or%20%E2%80%9Cvery%20important%E2%80%9D%20for%20their))**, reflecting the public’s awareness that these venues serve as de facto community centers. Pub industry leaders warn that, *“when pubs are ripped out of communities…the damage to social cohesion is incalculable,”* with loneliness levels in some hard-hit regions now comparable to the peak of the pandemic ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=rising%20levels%20of%20loneliness%20Philip,and%20action%20on%20spiralling%20regulatory)). These findings echo smaller-scale observations in other countries as well. Local news reports often note that when a longstanding coffee shop or diner closes, **nearby residents report fewer friendly exchanges and a loss of community “glue”** – which can lead to disengagement from local groups or activities over time. 

**In summary, the erosion of third places appears to have tangible negative effects on community social health.** Both broad statistical studies and on-the-ground reports confirm that **neighborhoods losing independent cafés, pubs, and similar venues often experience a decline in day-to-day social interactions, greater feelings of loneliness, and potentially lower civic participation** ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=reach%20far%20beyond%20losing%20a,effects%20can%20worsen%20health%20outcomes)) ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=levels,or%20%E2%80%9Cvery%20important%E2%80%9D%20for%20their)). This is why the cause of these closures matters: if the root problem is the **business environment (high rents, labor and supply costs)**, the solution might lie in economic relief or zoning reforms; if instead the main driver is **competition from delivery platforms and ghost kitchens**, policymakers might consider regulating those platforms or supporting brick-and-mortar businesses’ digital transition. In reality, the data suggest **both factors are at play** – meaning a **comprehensive approach will likely be needed** to preserve these crucial “third places” and the social fabric they support.  ([www.thedrinksbusiness.com](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/10/pub-closures-cause-an-increase-in-loneliness/#:~:text=other%20uses%20over%20the%20six,on%20from%20the%20survey%20results)) ([ibs.colorado.edu](https://ibs.colorado.edu/new-research-on-disappearing-third-places-warns-of-risks/#:~:text=reach%20far%20beyond%20losing%20a,effects%20can%20worsen%20health%20outcomes))
