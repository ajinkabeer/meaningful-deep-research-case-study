# Evidence (or Lack Thereof) Linking Frictionless Services to Loneliness

**Summary:** While public commentary and broad trends suggest a plausible link between the rise of frictionless services (self-checkouts, app-based ordering, drive-thrus) and increasing loneliness, **there is currently **no direct, causal **proof from peer-reviewed studies that reduced micro-interactions *cause* higher loneliness**. Researchers have well-documented that **casual face-to-face social contacts – even brief exchanges like chatting with a barista or cashier – can boost mood, belonging, and well-being** ([scispace.com](https://scispace.com/papers/is-efficiency-overrated-minimal-social-interactions-lead-to-5f3wks9hhm#:~:text=reap%20benefits%20by%20doing%20so%3B,stranger%20like%20a%20weak%20tie)) ([www.psychologytoday.com](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-power-of-belonging/202412/theres-a-high-cost-to-the-frictionless-customer-experience#:~:text=automation%20can%20have%20profound%20impacts,and%20even%20physical%20health%20challenges)). It is also clear that **opportunities for such interactions have declined in recent years** as retail and dining become more automated and “frictionless.” However, **the open research gap** is that we lack longitudinal or experimental studies isolating *the specific impact* of losing these **“transactional” micro-interactions** on loneliness or social isolation. Below we address each question in turn:

## 1. Longitudinal or Natural Experiments on Reduced Transactions and Loneliness

**No published longitudinal or natural-experiment study was found** that directly tests whether removing human service interactions causes a rise in loneliness. Existing evidence is **largely correlational and theoretical.** For example, **Dr. Michelle Lim (Swinburne University) noted in 2021 that “more research needs to be done to quantify the impacts of micro-interactions on loneliness”** ([www.abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2021-07-29/loneliness-micro-interactions-during-pandemic/100315922#:~:text=,Even%20if%20you%20have)), especially as everyday encounters were curtailed during COVID-19 lockdowns. 

That said, some **natural experiments and trend studies support a connection between declining in-person interaction and reduced well-being**:

- **Global Smartphone Adoption as a “Natural Experiment”:** Psychologist Jean Twenge and colleagues found a **sharp, worldwide rise in teen loneliness after 2012,** which closely coincided with **smartphone and social media adoption reaching a tipping point**. In a study of adolescents across 36 countries from 2012–2018, *loneliness levels increased in 36 of 37 countries*, and **the rise in loneliness correlated with increased smartphone/internet use rather than economic or family changes** ([www.chconline.org](https://www.chconline.org/resourcelibrary/teens-around-the-world-are-lonelier-than-a-decade-ago-the-reason-may-be-smartphones/#:~:text=loneliness%20and%20depression%20had%20been,paper%20said%3B%20it%20can%20also)). Before 2012 (when U.S. smartphone ownership passed 50%), teen loneliness and depression rates had been stable or falling for years; after that, both surged in the U.S., U.K., and Canada ([www.chconline.org](https://www.chconline.org/resourcelibrary/teens-around-the-world-are-lonelier-than-a-decade-ago-the-reason-may-be-smartphones/#:~:text=see,least%20half%20of%20teens%20had)). This suggests that **reducing face-to-face social contact (in this case via digital replacement) contributed to increased loneliness**, although these studies are observational (not controlled experiments).

- **COVID-19 & Social Interaction** (Another natural experiment): COVID lockdowns forcibly removed many daily in-person interactions, and loneliness rates spiked worldwide ([www.abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2021-07-29/loneliness-micro-interactions-during-pandemic/100315922#:~:text=old%20from%20Alphington%2C%20in%20Melbourne%27s,something%20else%20in%20your%20social)). For instance, half of Australians reported heightened loneliness during 2020’s lockdowns ([www.abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2021-07-29/loneliness-micro-interactions-during-pandemic/100315922#:~:text=old%20from%20Alphington%2C%20in%20Melbourne%27s,something%20else%20in%20your%20social)). This *implies* that losing routine interactions plays a role in loneliness. However, the pandemic was a complex event – isolating effects can’t be attributed to lost retail interactions alone.

- **Infrastructure Changes:** Beyond retail, one study leveraged a **transportation change as a natural experiment** – researchers found that altering public transit options had measurable **causal effects on social isolation and loneliness among older adults**, especially women ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1353829225001832#:~:text=Gendered%20impacts%20of%20public%20transport,and%20loneliness%20among%20older%20adults)). This indicates that **reducing opportunities for in-person contact (in this case, via public transport cuts) can increase social isolation**. By analogy, removing human contact points in stores or restaurants *could* have similar effects – but this specific hypothesis **has not yet been directly tested** in peer-reviewed research.

In summary, **no study to date has isolated the effect of specifically removing retail/dining micro-interactions on loneliness in a controlled way** (e.g. comparing towns or companies that adopt self-service vs. those that don’t). The evidence we have comes from **broad population trends and indirect experiments**, which strongly suggest a link but **stop short of proving direct causation**. Experts widely suspect the relationship is real – one review remarked that modern rises in loneliness likely stem from *“global societal shifts,” including “more technology use” and diminishing face-to-face connections* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9895855/#:~:text=of%20life%2C%20lonely%20consumers%201,range%20of%20negative%20health%20outcomes)) – but **this remains an open question for future research**.

## 2. Quantifying Daily Social Exchanges Lost to “Frictionless” Services (2010–2024)

**There is no single published figure for “daily micro-interactions lost per person” due to self-checkout, app ordering, and drive-thrus.** We did not find any researcher who has *directly measured how many fewer brief social exchanges (like cashier chats or line conversations) the average person has today compared to a decade ago*. **However, multiple data sources confirm substantial declines in daily face-to-face social contact over the 2010–2020 period**, which presumably include the kind of brief interactions in question:

- **Time Spent Socializing Has Declined:** Cross-national time-use surveys show that **the average person now spends only about 1 hour per day socializing in person, down from ~13 hours per week in the 1980s** – a roughly 50% reduction in social time over the past several decades ([www.socialconnectionguidelines.org](https://www.socialconnectionguidelines.org/en/evidence-briefs/how-much-social-time-do-we-need#:~:text=as%20much%20social%20time%20as,taken%20in%20relying%20on%20these)). In the U.S., **time spent in in-person social engagement has steadily fallen**. Analysis of the American Time Use Survey found that from 2003 to 2020, the average American’s **time spent with friends dropped by ~20 hours per month**, and time spent engaging with non-family *“others”* (neighbors, coworkers, acquaintances, etc.) **fell by ~10 hours per month** ([www.hhs.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-social-connection-graphic-trends.pdf#:~:text=minutes%20around%202019%20before%20a,of%2010%20hours%20per%20month)). That translates to roughly **20 fewer minutes per day of casual social interactions** with peripheral ties, on average, compared to the early 2000s. Meanwhile, **time spent physically alone increased by about 24 hours per month over the same period** ([www.hhs.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-social-connection-graphic-trends.pdf#:~:text=Steeper%20decline%20point%20begins%20slightly,satisfaction%2065%2060%2060%2055)). These trends reflect a broad reduction in everyday social encounters.

- **Shrinking Social Circles and Infrequent Interaction:** Survey data echo this pattern. A nationwide poll of older Americans (age 50–80) by the University of Michigan found that by 2023, **one-third (33%) of seniors reported social contact with friends, family or neighbors *less than once a week*** – up from 28% in 2018 ([mylifesite.net](https://mylifesite.net/blog/post/how-some-grocery-stores-are-helping-alleviate-loneliness#:~:text=reported%20by%20over%20a%20third,Go%20to%20the)). Likewise, the share of older adults *often feeling isolated* jumped to 34% in 2023 (from 27% in 2018) despite receding pandemic conditions ([mylifesite.net](https://mylifesite.net/blog/post/how-some-grocery-stores-are-helping-alleviate-loneliness#:~:text=Among%20the%20key%20findings%20of,One%20in%203%20respondents)) ([mylifesite.net](https://mylifesite.net/blog/post/how-some-grocery-stores-are-helping-alleviate-loneliness#:~:text=That%20figure%20was%20down%20from,neighbors%2C%20or%20family%20they%20do)). Among the general U.S. population, the U.S. Surgeon General’s office reports “overall societal declines in social connection,” including smaller social networks and fewer community interactions, in recent decades ([milled.com](https://milled.com/latimes/as-self-checkout-options-rise-so-does-loneliness--UVgI4u5hFYGlFN8#:~:text=and%20off%20you%20go,which%20%E2%80%9Caccounts%20for%20an%20estimated)). For example, the portion of Americans who said they have no close friends quadrupled from 3% in 1990 to 12% in 2021 ([www.psychologytoday.com](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-power-of-belonging/202412/theres-a-high-cost-to-the-frictionless-customer-experience#:~:text=carries%20more%20serious%20costs%20than,in%20the%20US%E2%80%94ages%2015%20to)). Such statistics don’t isolate brief transactions, but they **underscore a major reduction in everyday social interactions** in the same timeframe that frictionless, no-contact services have proliferated.

- **Frictionless Services Have Proliferated:** The rise of self-service retail since ~2010 is well documented. By 2022, nearly **40% of checkout lanes in U.S. grocery stores were self-checkouts** ([milled.com](https://milled.com/latimes/as-self-checkout-options-rise-so-does-loneliness--UVgI4u5hFYGlFN8#:~:text=self,industries%20have%20pushed%20for%20increased)). Surveys indicate **80% of Americans now say they *prefer* self-service kiosks over dealing with human cashiers** ([www.wcnc.com](https://www.wcnc.com/article/news/local/connect-the-dots/self-checkout-kiosks-americans-lonely/275-41b71a07-09b8-4778-ae45-40df3336caeb#:~:text=your%20own%20groceries%20has%20become,2021%20to%202031%2C%20and%20that)), and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects that **355,000 cashier jobs will vanish between 2021 and 2031** as automation expands ([www.wcnc.com](https://www.wcnc.com/article/news/local/connect-the-dots/self-checkout-kiosks-americans-lonely/275-41b71a07-09b8-4778-ae45-40df3336caeb#:~:text=traffic%20alerts%2C%20download%20the%20WCNC,Pandemic%20tax%20deadline)). Major fast-food chains are not only adding kiosks and drive-thru lanes, but even removing dine-in seating in new store designs. Starbucks announced in 2019 that it would **close many cafe locations in favor of “pickup-only” stores with no seating** – a trend accelerated by the pandemic ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=we%20really%20want,kindly%20informed%20me%20that%20she)) ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=complete%20and%20ready%20for%20pickup,I)). In one such Chicago Starbucks, **customers could only order via mobile app and there were *no* tables or chairs**; a walk-in customer looking for conversation (or even to pay cash) was politely turned away and left in frustration ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=we%20really%20want,kindly%20informed%20me%20that%20she)) ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=downloaded%20it%2C%20ordered%20and%20paid,is%20up%20for%20debate%2C%20but)). **These changes replace dozens of daily human contacts with zero-interaction alternatives** – eliminating the chat with a cashier or the chance to strike up small talk while waiting, eating, or shopping. As one journalist observed, **places “that used to be brimming with opportunities for community connection” have become “increasingly depersonalized,” giving a “creeping sense” that something vital has been lost ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=actually%20more%20%E2%80%9Cconvenient%E2%80%9D%20than%20a,shared%20spaces%20become%20increasingly%20depersonalized)).** 

**Bottom line:** No researcher has precisely quantified something like “X fewer social exchanges per person per day” due solely to self-checkout or app ordering. **However, multiple indicators show that **informal in-person interactions have declined significantly from 2010 to 2020**, in parallel with the spread of frictionless service technologies. It remains *plausible that millions of small daily chats have been lost*, even if we can’t yet put a precise number on it.

## 3. App-Order Users vs. Counter-Order Users: Differences in Loneliness or Social Connection?

**We found no specific scientific studies comparing frequent app/online-order users vs. traditional in-person customers on loneliness or social well-being measures.** No researcher appears to have published data splitting participants by their level of using mobile ordering, self-checkout, or drive-thru services and then measuring differences in **self-reported loneliness, social satisfaction, or sense of community.** This seems to be an **unexplored question** in the literature. 

It’s worth noting, however, that **psychological research strongly indicates that people gain emotional benefits from brief in-person interactions** – so it stands to reason that those who **opt for face-to-face service might enjoy slightly higher social fulfillment** (all else being equal) than those who always use automated or app-based service. For instance, a controlled experiment found that customers who took a moment to **chat with a coffee-shop barista felt significantly happier and more socially connected than those who rushed through a mobile or self-service transaction** ([scispace.com](https://scispace.com/papers/is-efficiency-overrated-minimal-social-interactions-lead-to-5f3wks9hhm#:~:text=reap%20benefits%20by%20doing%20so%3B,stranger%20like%20a%20weak%20tie)). Such minimal exchanges with familiar service staff can increase positive affect by fostering a **sense of being recognized and connected**, even when the interaction only lasts a few seconds ([vegoutmag.com](https://vegoutmag.com/lifestyle/d-psychology-says-people-who-refuse-to-use-self-checkout-at-the-grocery-arent-being-inefficient-theyre-protecting-one-of-the-few-small-daily-exchanges-modern-life-still-allows-the-brief-friendly-inter/#:~:text=daily%20exchanges%20modern%20life%20still,checkout%20machines)). Similarly, **a recent marketing study (Drexel University & U. of San Diego) found that shoppers who stick with human cashiers report higher store loyalty than those who use self-checkout**, which the authors attribute partly to the personal connection formed with staff ([vegoutmag.com](https://vegoutmag.com/lifestyle/d-psychology-says-people-who-refuse-to-use-self-checkout-at-the-grocery-arent-being-inefficient-theyre-protecting-one-of-the-few-small-daily-exchanges-modern-life-still-allows-the-brief-friendly-inter/#:~:text=convenience,interaction%20at%20checkout%20actually%20strengthens)). 

However, these findings only **imply potential social differences** – they do *not* directly prove that app users are lonelier or less satisfied. People who choose self-service may differ in personality or time constraints, which could also affect loneliness independently (for example, a very introverted or busy person might both use apps more *and* have lower social needs or opportunities). To establish a causal link, researchers would need to compare similar groups or communities where the only difference is the mode of service (app vs. in-person) and then measure loneliness or social well-being over time. **No such peer-reviewed study was identified** in our search, so any claims about app-users being *more lonely* than counter-order users remain speculative.

## 4. Links Between Removing In-Store Seating/Cashiers and Social Isolation Outcomes

**We found no direct peer-reviewed research that quantifies social isolation *before vs. after* the removal of in-store seating or cashier roles.** There is abundant discussion in media and public health circles about the likely social consequences of these changes, but **hard outcome data are lacking**.

Most evidence takes the form of **parallel trends and expert commentary** rather than controlled studies. For example, it is well-documented that many retailers and restaurants have remodeled to eliminate seating and human service counters in the 2010s and 2020s (often citing efficiency and pandemic-driven shifts) ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=complete%20and%20ready%20for%20pickup,I)). This “drive-thru-ification” has clear effects on behavior: a drive-thru or mobile pickup **“obviates the need to leave one’s car and…eliminates the limited possibility of social interaction”** that going inside a store or restaurant formerly offered ([www.strongtowns.org](https://www.strongtowns.org/journal/2024-5-24-how-modern-america-is-optimized-for-loneliness-misery-and-poor-health#:~:text=Starbucks%2C%20like%20many%20suburban%20chains%2C,often%20whilst%20drowning%20them%20in)). In other words, **removing seating and cashiers *by design reduces casual social contact***. It stands to reason that *if* those contacts are truly a source of social connection, removing them would worsen loneliness. **What’s missing is definitive proof of downstream effects** (e.g. a measured rise in local loneliness or isolation after such a change).

Still, some **indirect evidence and initiatives link these changes to social isolation:** The U.S. Surgeon General’s 2023 report on loneliness highlights the broad decline in “social participation” and face-to-face interactions in community spaces, warning that our society has **“dismantled” many structures that used to bring people together, with serious health costs ([milled.com](https://milled.com/latimes/as-self-checkout-options-rise-so-does-loneliness--UVgI4u5hFYGlFN8#:~:text=and%20off%20you%20go,which%20%E2%80%9Caccounts%20for%20an%20estimated)).** And in the Netherlands, concern over lost social contact in retail led to a government-backed experiment: the **“Kletskassa” or *“chat checkout”* program, which added **cashier lanes for **customers who crave conversation**. Starting in 2019, the Dutch supermarket chain Jumbo introduced **125 of these slow, talk-friendly checkouts** as part of a national campaign to combat loneliness ([www.salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2024/01/22/ghost-kitchens-and-digital-cafes-how-our-shared-spaces-are-losing-their-humanity/#:~:text=connection%20when%20possible,global%20loneliness%20epidemic%2C%20and%20the)). This real-world initiative was driven by evidence that *some people (especially seniors) rely on brief interactions at grocery stores to avoid loneliness*. Its existence underscores the perceived link between removing human cashiers and increasing social isolation – even if academic research hasn’t yet formally measured this effect.

**In summary, current literature provides strong correlational evidence and expert opinion that the removal of everyday “social friction” (like in-person service interactions and shared public spaces) is *contributing* to rising loneliness – but no study has conclusively proven a direct causal relationship.** The lack of longitudinal or experimental studies on **transaction-specific social contact loss** means the core thesis (that eliminating human interactions in services *directly causes* higher loneliness) remains **unproven**. We have ample data showing parallel trends – e.g. **fewer daily social exchanges alongside greater loneliness and social isolation** ([www.hhs.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-social-connection-graphic-trends.pdf#:~:text=Steeper%20decline%20point%20begins%20slightly,satisfaction%2065%2060%2060%2055)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S135382921930735X#:~:text=A%20rising%20number%20of%20Americans,Talen%20and%20Jeong%2C%202019)) – and a well-founded theoretical basis for concern. Yet, without targeted studies we can only say the trends are **consistent with** the hypothesis. This evidentiary gap weakens any policy argument to **“restore micro-interactions”** as a loneliness intervention. To strengthen it, researchers would need to conduct studies (e.g. comparing communities or companies with vs. without frictionless services, or tracking people who shift from in-person to app-based behaviors) and measure resulting changes in loneliness or well-being. **Until such research is done, the link between frictionless service adoption and the loneliness epidemic – while plausible – remains a correlation rather than confirmed causation.** ([www.abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2021-07-29/loneliness-micro-interactions-during-pandemic/100315922#:~:text=,Even%20if%20you%20have)) ([www.chconline.org](https://www.chconline.org/resourcelibrary/teens-around-the-world-are-lonelier-than-a-decade-ago-the-reason-may-be-smartphones/#:~:text=increased%20in%20the%20United%20States%2C,they%20can%20have%20a%20negative))
